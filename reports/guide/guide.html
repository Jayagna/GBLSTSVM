Fuzzy GBLSTSVM: what we have done, and what it means

A plain-language guide for Jayagna · 20 October 2026, after round 7 (the GBFTSVM comparison) and the midsem deck · written for your understanding, not for publication

The short version

  • The goal is a new fuzzy membership function that makes GBLSTSVM clearly better, strong enough for a paper.
  • We built a fair test bench first. 21 datasets, 5 random splits each, every setting tuned on training data only, and 0 to 30% of training labels deliberately flipped to test robustness.
  • The first four fuzzy functions (T1, T2, T4, T5) change GBLSTSVM by less than 1 point. That is too small for a paper.
  • The cause is not the fuzzy function. GBLSTSVM deletes every granular ball that holds a single point. That throws away 35 to 40% of the training data, mostly near the class boundary, and costs it 4 to 7 points against plain LSTSVM, which keeps every point.
  • Most fuzzy functions cannot tell a mislabelled point from a correct one. The entropy-based one is worse than a coin toss. A function that asks “do my neighbours share my label?” (T7) finds about 90% of them.
  • In round 4, T7 lifted the ball model above GBLSTSVM at every noise level, and at 30% noise it nearly caught plain LSTSVM. The same idea on raw points (classic fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM, an already-known method) was the best of everything under noise.
  • GBLSTSVM’s robustness to noise comes from deleting points. At 20% noise it deletes 86% of the flipped labels, but also 53% of the correct points.
  • The MATLAB suite on your Desktop already contains overlap- and neighbour-based weights (OBRU, CBCM, Topo), so our T4 is not new even within your group. It suggested a better ball builder (ADPGBG); in round 5 that did no better than simply keeping the single points.
  • Where this leaves us: in the linear model, training on balls always costs 1 to 4 points against training on every point, whichever builder or weight we use. Within the ball family, keeping single points plus T7 beats GBLSTSVM clearly: +4.1 points on clean data and +3.4 at 30% noise.
  • Round 6 tested the kernel (curved-boundary) version, where balls were supposed to pay off. There, keep + T7 is only 0.1 to 0.9 points above kernel GBLSTSVM, not significant. Ball models do help at 30% noise and train in seconds on 70,000 points, where kernel LSTSVM runs out of memory. But a standard shortcut that needs no balls (reduced-kernel LSTSVM) is about as fast and 0.5 to 3 points more accurate.
  • Round 7 added GBFTSVM, the closest published model (fuzzy weights on granular balls in a twin SVM). Under fair tuning it is no better than GBLSTSVM, its fuzzy scores change almost nothing, and keep + T7 beats it by 3.7 to 5.6 points at every noise level. Its paper reports much higher numbers because it tunes on the test set; doing the same in our runs adds 9 to 15 points.
  • You chose the analysis paper (option C in section 5): where granular balls help twin SVMs and where they do not, with keep + T7 as its proposed fix. Section 6 has the final numbers it will use, now averaged over 10 splits instead of 5.

1. The background in plain words

1.1 What LSTSVM does

A normal support vector machine (SVM) looks for one straight line (in many dimensions, a hyperplane) that separates two classes. A twin SVM looks for two lines instead. Line 1 runs as close as possible to the points of class +1 while staying at least a distance of 1 away from the points of class −1. Line 2 does the opposite. A new point is given the class whose line it lies closer to.

The least squares version (LSTSVM) replaces the hard “stay at least 1 away” rules with squared penalties. Training then becomes solving two small systems of linear equations (one row and column per feature, plus one), so it is very fast and has an exact answer. Two numbers, c1 and c2, set how hard each line is pushed away from the other class.

1.2 What GBLSTSVM adds: granular balls

GBLSTSVM (Tanveer, Sharma, Quadir and Sajid, Pattern Recognition 2025) first compresses the training data into granular balls. It starts with all points in one ball and splits it with k-means into k smaller balls. Any ball in which fewer than 98.5% of the points share one label is split again, and so on. Each final ball is summarised by three things: its centre, its radius (the average distance of its points from the centre) and its label (the majority label).

The twin lines are then trained on the ball centres instead of the points, with the radius added to the margin (“stay 1 + r away”). The paper claims two benefits: far fewer items to train on, so it is faster, and robustness to wrong labels, because a single wrong label inside a big ball is outvoted by its neighbours.

One detail turns out to matter a lot: balls that contain only one point are deleted before training (delbals=2 in gen_ball.py).

1.3 What a fuzzy membership is

A fuzzy membership is a number between 0 and 1 attached to each training item, saying how much we trust it. An item with membership 1 counts fully; an item with 0.1 counts only one tenth when the model pays a penalty for having it on the wrong side. The idea comes from the fuzzy SVM of Lin and Wang (2002): give outliers and mislabelled points low memberships so the lines do not bend towards them.

Our project puts such a number on each ball. In the code it is one multiplication: the penalty weight of each ball (the “slack” weight) becomes class weight × membership. Everything else in GBLSTSVM stays the same, so any improvement can be credited to the membership function.

2. How we test, and why it is stricter than the original code

The original main.py tested each dataset on one random split and picked c1, c2 and k by the best accuracy on the test set. Picking the best of many tries on the test data itself makes every method look better than it is and makes small differences look real. A reviewer would reject a paper built on that. So before testing any idea, we built a stricter test bench (experiments/run_fuzzy_cv.py):

  • 21 datasets from your Data/LSTSVM_benchmarks folder, with 194 to 1,484 samples and 3 to 60 features. Nine are strongly imbalanced: the smaller class is between 5.7% (abalone9-18) and 16% (new-thyroid1) of the data.
  • 5 random 70/30 train/test splits per dataset, each keeping the same class mix in both parts.
  • All settings chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on the 70% training part only: c1 and c2 from 0.001 to 1000, k from 2 to 6, class weighting on or off, and the fuzzy function’s own parameter. The 30% test part is touched once, at the very end.
  • Label noise: before training, 10%, 20% (from round 4 on, 30%) of the training labels are flipped at random. The test labels stay correct. This tests the paper’s robustness claim directly, and robustness is where fuzzy memberships are supposed to help.
  • Main score: balanced accuracy, the average of the accuracy on class +1 and the accuracy on class −1. On a dataset that is 95% class −1, a model that always answers −1 scores 95% accuracy but only 50% balanced accuracy, so plain accuracy would hide failures on the small class. We also record accuracy, G-mean, F1 and ROC-AUC.
  • Statistics: the Friedman test asks whether the methods differ at all across the 21 datasets. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test asks, for each method against GBLSTSVM, whether it wins consistently across datasets rather than only on average. A p-value under 0.05 means a pattern that consistent would rarely appear by chance.

Round 1: four fuzzy functions on GBLSTSVM’s balls no real gain

Question
Does a fuzzy weight on the balls improve GBLSTSVM?
What we did
Tested T1 centre distance, T2 neighbour entropy, T4 overlap and T5 density at 0, 10 and 20% label noise, with plain LSTSVM as a reference.
Result
All four stayed within 1 point of GBLSTSVM (73.4% balanced accuracy, versus 73.5 to 74.2%). Only T2 on clean data passed the significance test (p = 0.044), and it lost that edge under noise. T4 and T5 were significantly worse under noise (p = 0.010 and 0.049). Plain LSTSVM beat every ball-based model by 4 to 7 points.
What it means
The weights were not the bottleneck. Something in the ball step itself was costing accuracy, and no weight on top of it could win that back.

Round 2: why does the ball step cost so much? found the cause

Question
What does GBLSTSVM lose when it turns points into balls?
What we did
Counted what is deleted, then tested: keeping single-point balls (GB-keep), keeping them with a tunable lower weight (T6), setting every radius to 0 (to see if the radius matters), and T2 and T4 on top of GB-keep.
Result
35 to 40% of training points end up alone in a ball and are deleted, mostly at the class boundary and in the small class (Figure 1). Keeping them raised balanced accuracy from 73.4 to 77.1% (p = 0.001), mostly by catching more of the small class. T6 weighting added another 0.6 points, not significant. Fuzzy weights on top added nothing. Setting all radii to 0 changed nothing, so the radius is not what makes GBLSTSVM work. LSTSVM was still about 3 points ahead.
What it means
The paper’s “delete single-point balls” step is the main weakness of GBLSTSVM, bigger than anything a fuzzy function could fix on its own.

Round 3: balls only as a weighting tool no gain

Question
If training on every point is best, can the balls still help by telling us how much to trust each point?
What we did
Trained on all points, like LSTSVM, but gave each point the weight of the ball it falls in, using three ball weights (size, overlap, entropy).
Result
Slightly below LSTSVM at every noise level (0.2 to 0.9 points), and never significantly different from it (all p > 0.1).
What it means
These weights do almost nothing. That raised a sharper question: are they pointing at the noisy points at all?

Diagnostic: do the weights actually find the mislabelled points? key insight

We flipped 10% and 20% of the training labels and checked whether the flipped points got lower weights than the correct ones. The score is ROC-AUC: 0.5 means the weight is no better than a coin toss at ranking flipped points below correct ones, and 1.0 means every flipped point gets a lower weight than every correct point.

Why does entropy fail so badly? A flipped point usually sits deep inside the other class. All its neighbours share one label (just not its own), so its neighbourhood looks perfectly pure, entropy is zero, and it gets full trust. Meanwhile a correctly labelled point on the boundary has a mixed neighbourhood and is punished. Entropy measures how mixed an area is; finding wrong labels needs whether the neighbours agree with this point’s own label. That is the idea behind T7.

Round 4: T7 neighbour agreement helps under heavy noise

Question
Does a weight that actually finds flipped labels (T7) make the ball models robust to noise?
What we did
T7 gives each ball the share of its k nearest balls (counted by points) that carry the same label, raised to a power γ. Both k (5 or 10) and γ (1 or 3) were chosen by cross-validation. We tried it on GBLSTSVM’s balls, on all balls (single points kept), and on points (each point takes its ball’s value), against classic fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM, which is the same idea on raw points and a known method (Keller et al., 1985). What it means

On GBLSTSVM’s own balls, T7 does nothing, because GBLSTSVM already deletes most flipped labels. I measured this on 12 datasets at 20% noise: 86% of the flipped points end up alone in a ball and are deleted, but so are 53% of the correct points. Deleting single-point balls is a crude noise filter. It explains why GBLSTSVM loses so little as noise grows, and also why it starts so low.

On all balls, T7 helps, and more as noise grows. Over simply keeping the single points it adds 0.2 to 0.8 points up to 20% noise and 2.4 points at 30% (p = 0.029). The ball model still trails LSTSVM: by 2 to 3 points up to 20% noise, and by 0.6 points at 30%, which is no longer significant.

Neighbour agreement works best on points. Classic fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM beats LSTSVM by 1.0 point at 20% noise (p = 0.019, better on 15 of 21 datasets) and by 1.9 points at 30% (p = 0.009). Because that method is already known, it is a baseline for us, not a contribution.

Point weights taken from balls do just as well. Giving each point the T7 value of its ball beats LSTSVM by 2.1 points at 30% noise (p = 0.016, better on 16 of 21), level with the classic method. It searches for neighbours among balls instead of among all points, which could make it cheaper on large data (not measured yet). That is a possible angle, but a modest one on its own.

Bottom line: squeezing points into k-means balls costs 2 to 3 points, more than any weight wins back. The ball builder has to get better before a ball-level fuzzy weight can be the paper’s contribution. That is what round 5 (ADPGBG) tests.

ADPGBG balls are about as good as simply keeping the single points. They were the best ball model at 10% noise, still 1.3 points below LSTSVM (p = 0.079), and slightly worse elsewhere. Balls do not make the fuzzy weights cheaper either, at these sizes. On the large NDC datasets (5,000 to 20,000 points), building the balls took 0.3 to 0.9 seconds, while finding the 10 nearest neighbours of every point took 0.1 to 0.7 seconds (one split, a quick check).

Round 6: the kernel version the fix adds little here

Question
Do the ball models catch up with LSTSVM, and does keep + T7 still help, when the boundary may curve?
What we did

So far every model drew straight lines. A kernel model can draw curved ones. It picks a set of reference points (the kernel columns) and describes every point by how similar it is to each reference point: close gives a similarity near 1, far gives near 0 (the RBF kernel). The twin lines are then drawn in that similarity space, which bends them in the original space. How fast the similarity falls with distance is one more setting, tuned by cross-validation like the others.

Kernel LSTSVM uses every training point as a reference point, so its equations have one row and column per point. Kernel GBLSTSVM uses the ball centres, so it has far fewer. We ran kernel GBLSTSVM, kernel keep (single-point balls kept), kernel keep + T7, kernel LSTSVM and kernel fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM through the same 21 datasets, splits and tuning, at 0, 20 and 30% noise.

What it means

The fix that worked in the linear model barely matters here. Keep + T7 is 0.9, 0.1 and 0.6 points above kernel GBLSTSVM at 0, 20 and 30% noise, none of it significant (p ≥ 0.5). With a curved boundary, deleting the single-point balls costs much less than with a straight one.

Ball models are more robust to heavy noise than kernel LSTSVM. At 30% noise, keep + T7 beats kernel LSTSVM by 2.3 points (p = 0.026, better on 15 of 21 datasets) and kernel GBLSTSVM by 1.6 (p = 0.08). On clean data they trail it by 1.7 to 2.6 points (p ≤ 0.04).

But the known fuzzy-kNN weights do better still. Kernel fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM beats keep + T7 by 2.0 to 2.7 points at every noise level (p ≤ 0.022).

Under noise, kept balls are hardly a compression. On clean data the kept balls number about 40% of the points; at 20 to 30% noise, 72 to 80%, because flipped labels split the balls into single points.

Balls do make kernel training feasible on large data, but they are not the only way. Reduced-kernel LSTSVM, a standard trick that needs no balls, is in the same speed range and is 0.5 to 3 points more accurate on clean data. A paper claiming speed for kernel GBLSTSVM would have to beat it, and on these data it does not.

Under noise, kept balls stop being small. Flipped labels turn most points into single-point balls, so keep + T7 loses its speed edge. From 28,000 training points up they exceed the limit. GBLSTSVM’s deletion keeps its ball count low (about 14% of the points) and acts as a noise filter: from 28,000 points up it is only 0.1 to 0.6 points below the best model, reduced-kernel fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM, while training 3 to 8 times faster (it also uses fewer reference points). This is the one place where the balls clearly earn their keep, and it comes from deleting points, not from a fuzzy weight.

Round 7: GBFTSVM, the closest published model keep + T7 beats it

Question
GBFTSVM (Zhao, Ding, Miao and Lang, arXiv 2408.00699) already puts a fuzzy weight on granular balls in a twin SVM. How does it do under our fair test, and does our fix beat it?
What it is

GBFTSVM differs from GBLSTSVM in two ways. First, its twin lines come from a classic twin SVM instead of the least squares version: a ball of the other class only costs something when it comes closer to a line than a set margin, whereas in GBLSTSVM every ball of the other class pulls on the line. That makes the model a little slower to train (it solves a small optimisation problem per line instead of one set of equations) but it is the more common design.

Second, each ball gets a score built from a Pythagorean fuzzy set: a membership µ (how close the ball’s centre is to the centre of its class, exactly our T1) and a non-membership ν (which grows when the ball is impure, that is, contains points of the other class). The two are combined into one “closeness” score. The paper uses µ alone for pure balls and the combined score for impure ones, which it treats as boundary balls and trusts more.

What we did

I implemented GBFTSVM from the PDF you saved (gbftsvm.py) and ran it on the same 21 datasets, 10 splits and 0 to 30% noise, with the paper’s grid for its two settings (2−5 to 25) chosen by cross-validation, and the same choice of k and class weighting as GBLSTSVM. To see which of its parts matter, I also ran it without the fuzzy scores (GBTWSVM, the authors’ earlier model), with single-point balls kept, and on ADPGBG balls, which like the paper’s own ball builder never overlap the other class.

Two technical points. The paper solves each line with a general-purpose optimiser (L-BFGS-B); on some of our datasets that optimiser stopped far from the true solution (on ger, lines 100% off), so I solve the same problem exactly with a dedicated SVM solver and checked it against a third, independent solver. And for every run I also recorded what tuning on the test set would have reported, to compare with the paper’s own numbers.

What it means

Under fair tuning, GBFTSVM is no better than GBLSTSVM. It is 1.8, 1.1 and 2.7 points behind it at 0, 10 and 20% noise and level with it at 30% (+0.1). Only the gap at 20% stays significant once corrected for multiple tests (Holm p = 0.01).

Its fuzzy scores add nothing. GBFTSVM and the same model without scores (GBTWSVM) differ by +0.1, −0.2, −1.2 and +0.7 points at the four noise levels, never significantly after the Holm correction. The reason is simple: on a pure ball, the Pythagorean score is just the centre distance (our T1, which also did nothing in round 1), and with k-means balls over 99% of the balls are pure. The part that is supposed to be new almost never switches on.

Our main finding carries over to this model. Keeping the single-point balls lifts GBFTSVM by 4.5, 3.3, 4.0 and 1.8 points at the four noise levels, the same kind of gain it gave GBLSTSVM. So the deletion step, not the choice of fuzzy weight, is what holds granular-ball twin SVMs back.

Keep + T7 beats GBFTSVM clearly: by 5.1, 3.7, 5.6 and 4.7 points at 0, 10, 20 and 30% noise (Holm-corrected p ≤ 0.005 at every level). This is the comparison a reviewer will ask for first, and it now exists.

Why the paper’s numbers are so much higher. The paper chooses its settings by the best result on the test data, as the original GBLSTSVM code does. When I do the same with our runs, the scores rise by 9 to 15 points of balanced accuracy, for GBLSTSVM and GBFTSVM alike, with no change to the models at all. On the six datasets both studies use, the paper reports 92.5% accuracy for GBFTSVM on average; our runs reach 81.4% even with that optimistic tuning and 74.0% with fair tuning. On sonar it reports 97%, while our test-tuned run reaches 77.9% and plain LSTSVM on all points about 70% (balanced accuracy). Part of the gap may come from its larger training sets (ten-fold CV trains on 90% of the data, ours on 70%) and its different ball builder, but in our runs tuning on the test set alone is worth 9 to 15 points. This belongs in the paper’s point about protocol: published gains of fuzzy granular-ball models can be artefacts of tuning on the test set.

4. What we found in the MATLAB suite on your Desktop

The GBLSTSVM_MATLAB folder (by Garv Jindal, a MATLAB port of the Toast24/GBLSTSVM-Ex repository) already contains five weighting variants and a template for testing new fuzzy functions:

VariantWhat the weight measuresRelation to our topics
CWClass balance only (rarer class weighted up)Same as our “imbalance on” setting, tuned in every run
CRBall purity and radiusWith our balls, purity is almost always exactly 1, so only the radius part can tell balls apart (we checked purity early on)
CBCM“Competition” with the other class among neighbours × support from same-class neighboursDifferent from T7; see below
TopoNeighbourhood ambiguity × reciprocal links to the other classDifferent from T7; see below
OBRUOverlap with balls of the other classEssentially our T4, with a slightly different formula

What this means for us:

  1. T4 is not new, even inside your group, and our data says it hurts under noise anyway.
  2. CBCM and Topo are not the same as T7. Their competition and ambiguity terms peak when a ball’s neighbours are split 50/50, so they give the most weight to boundary balls and low weight both to clean interior balls and to balls surrounded by the other class. T7 is one-directional: the more neighbours disagree with a ball’s label, the lower its weight. That one-directional rule is what finds flipped labels in Figure 4.
  3. Its benchmark cannot back a paper claim. It uses one synthetic dataset (two moons), one split and fixed c1 = 1, c2 = 0.1. If you want those variants in the paper, we can run them through our 21-dataset test bench.
  4. Its clearest result agrees with ours: the ball generator matters more than the weights. With balls from the ADPGBG generator (Liao et al., 2025) its baseline reached 95% accuracy, against 71% with k-means balls. ADPGBG grows each ball outwards until it reaches the nearest point of the other class, so boundary points stay in, and it drops only points that have no same-class neighbour nearby (likely outliers).

I ported ADPGBG to Python (adpgbg.py) and tested it on all 21 datasets in round 5 (section 3). With real benchmarks and fair tuning, it was no better than simply keeping the single points.

5. What it all means, and the decision for you

The honest picture after six rounds

  • In the linear model, training on balls costs 1 to 4 points of balanced accuracy against training on every point (LSTSVM), whichever ball builder or fuzzy weight we use.
  • In the linear model there is a clear, significant improvement over GBLSTSVM: keep the single-point balls and weight every ball by neighbour agreement (T7). That gains 4.1 points on clean data (p < 0.001) and 3.4 points at 30% noise (p = 0.046), from 5 splits; section 6 has the final 10-split numbers. In the kernel model the same fix gains under 1 point, not significant.
  • In the kernel model, ball models beat kernel LSTSVM under heavy noise (+2.3 points at 30%) but trail it on clean data, and the known fuzzy-kNN LSTSVM beats them at every noise level.
  • Balls make kernel training fast on large data, but reduced-kernel LSTSVM, which needs no balls, is about as fast and more accurate. So speed cannot be the paper’s main claim.
  • Findings that hold whatever the framing: GBLSTSVM’s robustness to noise comes from deleting points (86% of flipped labels, but also 53% of correct ones); entropy-style memberships measure the wrong thing for noise; and the original code’s test-set tuning makes all of this hard to see.

Glossary

Balanced accuracy
Average of the accuracy on each class. Fair on imbalanced data, where plain accuracy rewards ignoring the small class.
Cross-validation (5-fold)
Split the training data into 5 parts; train on 4 and score on the 5th, five times. Used to choose settings without touching the test set.
Label noise
Training points whose class label is wrong. We create it on purpose by flipping a share of training labels.
ROC-AUC
The chance that a randomly chosen positive case is ranked above a randomly chosen negative case. 0.5 is a coin toss, 1.0 is perfect.
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
Checks whether one method beats another consistently across the 21 datasets, not just on average.
Friedman test
Checks whether a group of methods differ at all across datasets, using their ranks.
Granular ball
A group of nearby training points summarised by a centre, a radius and a majority label.
Purity
The share of points in a ball that carry its majority label. GBLSTSVM splits until purity is at least 98.5%.
k
How many pieces each k-means split creates when building balls.
c1, c2
How strongly each of the two lines is pushed away from the other class.
Slack weight
How much the model pays when an item of the other class sits too close to a line. The fuzzy membership multiplies this.
Fuzzy membership
A trust score from 0 to 1 for each training item or ball.
LSTSVM / GBLSTSVM
Least squares twin SVM on raw points / on granular balls.
FKNN-LSTSVM
LSTSVM with the classic fuzzy k-nearest-neighbour membership on raw points. Our strongest point-level competitor.
ADPGBG
An adaptive ball builder that grows each ball up to the nearest point of the other class and drops isolated points.
Kernel (RBF)
Describes each point by its similarity to a set of reference points, so the twin lines can curve.
Holm correction
Raises p-values when one method is tested against several others, so that luck across many tests is not mistaken for an effect.
Critical-difference diagram
Methods placed by average rank; a bar joins methods that are not significantly different.
GBFTSVM
Granular-ball fuzzy twin SVM (Zhao et al., 2024): a classic twin SVM on granular balls with a Pythagorean fuzzy score per ball. The closest published model to ours.
Pythagorean fuzzy set
Gives each item a membership µ and a non-membership ν with µ² + ν² ≤ 1, so doubt can be expressed as well as trust.
Test-set tuning
Choosing settings by the best score on the test data. It makes every method look better than it is; we tune by cross-validation on training data instead.
Reduced kernel
A kernel model that uses only a random subset of the training points as reference points, to save memory and time.
